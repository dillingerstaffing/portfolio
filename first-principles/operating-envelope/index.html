The Operating Envelope.

Performance, power, and thermal: the three constraints every level of the stack lives inside. The main page shows what each level is assembled from. This page is the other cut: what the assembly must survive, who pays when it doesn't, and why the stack looks the way it does.

E1

The triad

Performance costs power, power becomes heat, heat caps performance. Three constraints, one loop, no level escapes it.

PERFORMANCE
Throughput and latency, measured in ops/sec and seconds. The ML engineer pays in time to train: a run that should take a week takes a month, salaries burn, the idea goes stale. The user pays in latency. The business pays in throughput per dollar of fleet.
POWER
Watts in, dollars out. The data-center operator pays the electricity bill, the largest operating cost of the building; one percent of efficiency is millions. The OEM pays in PSU and rack-feed sizing: a hard watts-per-box budget. The mobile user pays in battery.
THERMAL
Heat out, degrees on the die. The chip designer pays in power density: it decides how much silicon may switch at once. The OEM pays in heatsinks, fans, skin temperature, noise. The operator pays in cooling, which is poured in concrete once and can never be retrofitted.

Why these three and only these: each has its own unit, its own failure, and its own payer. Together they cover every way a machine can disappoint its stakeholders.

E2

The wall

L5 and L6 are not fashion. They are what the envelope forced: when clocks could no longer rise, the only road to more performance was more parallelism per watt, then less general hardware per watt.

DENNARD SCALING
Smaller transistors once meant faster and cooler at once. Around 2005 that broke: shrinking kept adding transistors, but power density stopped falling. Raising the clock further would have melted the die, so the clock stopped rising.
THE RESPONSE
First multicore (L5): more work per watt through parallelism instead of frequency. Then specialization (L6): delete the general-purpose control and spend the power budget on arithmetic alone. Each step is the stack re-assembling itself inside a tighter envelope.
DARK SILICON
At modern densities the whole die cannot switch at once. Some fraction must stay dark, always. This is a thermal verdict on a logic design: you own transistors you are never allowed to turn on.
E3

What wrong looks like

Every envelope violation has a signature you can observe. Learn the signatures and you can read a sick machine like a chart.

TOO HOT
The signature is the sawtooth: temperature rides the limit while throughput oscillates as the chip protects itself by slowing down. Sustained heat ends in emergency shutdown, an outage. Chronic heat shows up months later as shortened life and RMAs. In batteries it shows up as fire.
TOO MUCH POWER
The signature is the cliff: draw exceeds delivery and the breaker or the PSU overcurrent trips, instant outage. The slow version is stranded capacity: the rack power cap means racks that can never be filled, capex that never earns.
TOO SLOW
The signature is the queue that never drains: latency SLAs breached, users gone, contract penalties. In training, the run outlives its budget and dies with nothing to show: money burned, no model.
E4

The demand

The economics point at ever-larger models, so the envelope is always under assault. This is why accelerators exist and why the pressure never lets up.

SCALING LAWS
Loss falls as a power law in parameters, data, and compute, over seven orders of magnitude. The compute-optimal move is counterintuitive: train very large models on modest data and stop before convergence. Big models beat big data.
MORE IS DIFFERENT
Smooth loss curves hide discontinuous capability jumps. Demand arrives as a step function the envelope was never sized for: the fleet that was adequate on Tuesday is inadequate on Wednesday.
WHY IT MATTERS HERE
Every point on that curve is bought in watts and cooled in concrete. L6 exists where the demand curve meets the supply curve: the industry builds accelerators because the economics keep ordering larger models and the envelope dictates what may actually run.
